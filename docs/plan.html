Section 01

What's missing from the brief

Your brief is unusually complete. These are the things that aren't in it and that a catering business gets hurt by — most of them are business rules, not features.

  1. 01

    Lead time and a cut-off

    Without it, someone orders a buffet for forty people at 11pm for tomorrow morning and you're contractually on the hook. Catering needs a rule like "orders close 48 hours before delivery." It may differ per dish. This is the single most important missing rule.

  2. 02

    Daily capacity

    Your kitchen has a ceiling. The site must stop taking orders for a date once you've hit it, or a good day becomes a disaster. I'll build a simple cap: maximum orders per day, plus the ability to block out dates entirely.

  3. 03

    Minimum order value

    Catering delivery only makes sense above a certain basket size. Most catering sites set one. Yours is currently unstated.

  4. 04

    Serving guidance on every dish

    "Serves 8–10 people" is the piece of information your Instagram DMs are probably full of questions about. Putting it on each product is the highest-value thing on the whole menu page, and it needs to be a real database field, not a sentence buried in the description.

  5. 05

    A deposit rule

    Large catering orders are usually confirmed with a deposit, because a cancelled 60-person order is a real loss. Decide whether you want one, and from what order value.

  6. 06

    WhatsApp, not email

    In Egypt, email confirmations mostly go unread. The single most useful notification is a WhatsApp message. I can do a working version of this in the MVP for free — no API, no monthly cost — using a "Confirm on WhatsApp" button that opens a chat with your order summary already typed out. The automated version comes later.

  7. 07

    Arabic

    A large share of your customers may prefer Arabic. Building it now roughly doubles the content work; building it later onto a site that never planned for it is a rewrite. My recommendation: launch in English, but structure the text and layout for Arabic from day one so adding it is a content job, not a rebuild.

  8. 08

    Allergens

    You're feeding gatherings. One nut allergy is a serious incident. A simple allergen field per dish, shown on the product page, is cheap now.

  9. 09

    How you find out an order arrived

    An admin dashboard nobody is looking at is worse than a DM. You need a push to your phone. MVP answer: every new order also lands as a WhatsApp message to your business number, and the dashboard opens on your phone browser.

  10. 10

    Pickup

    Some customers will collect. It's nearly free to support and removes the delivery fee question entirely for those orders.

  11. 11

    A cancellation policy

    Needed before you take a single deposit. It also needs somewhere to live on the site.

  12. 12

    A kitchen prep view

    Not in your brief, but the thing you'll use most. One screen: pick a date, see every dish ordered for it, totalled across all orders — "42 portions of X, 9 trays of Y." That's your shopping list and your morning. I want it in the MVP.

Section 03

The customer journey

From the link in your Instagram bio to a confirmed order in your kitchen. Nine steps, and I've noted where people usually drop out.

Instagram

The link in your bio

One link, straight to the site. Tagged so you can see how many orders came from Instagram versus anywhere else.

Home

Ten seconds to understand

Photography of your actual food, "Food Made With Passion", and immediately clear that this is catering for gatherings and hosting — not single-portion delivery. Order Now is the primary button, View Menu the secondary.

Menu

Browse by category

Main Courses, Side Dishes, Salads and anything else you add. Photo, name, serving size, price per card. Unavailable items shown but clearly marked, never silently hidden — seeing them builds the menu in the customer's head.

Product

Choose and configure

Full description, what it serves, allergens, and any choices the dish requires — rice, sauce, side, filling. Required choices must be made before the button unlocks. Optional add-ons priced. Quantity respects the minimum. The price updates live as they choose. Special instructions box.

Cart

Survives leaving

Saved in their browser, so closing the tab or going back to Instagram doesn't lose it. Every choice they made is shown on the line, quantities editable, running subtotal.

Checkout

When and where — before who

Date and time slot come first, because they're the ones that can fail. Only dates that respect your lead time, aren't blocked, and aren't already full can be picked. Then area — which sets the delivery fee — then address, name, mobile, notes. Egyptian mobile format validated. Minimum order value checked here.

Payment

How they'll pay

For launch: cash on delivery and/or bank transfer, whichever you enable. They choose, we record it, nothing is charged online. Built as a separate layer so card payments slot in later without touching any of the ordering flow.

Submit

Checked again, then saved

The date is re-verified at the moment of submitting — someone else may have taken the last slot while they were typing. Then the order is written to the database and given a number like TD-260828-04, short enough to say out loud on the phone.

Confirmed

The page that ends the anxiety

Order number, full summary, total, delivery date and slot, address, payment method and status, current order status — and a Confirm on WhatsApp button that opens a chat with you, pre-filled. That button is what replaces the DM, and it means you can still talk to every customer.

Section 05

How dishes with choices get modelled

You asked me not to turn every variation into its own product, and you're right. Here are the rules I'll apply to your menu once I can read it — please tell me if any of them contradict how you actually sell.

The four rules

Every line on your menu becomes exactly one of these:

It becomesWhenBehaves like
A productIt can be ordered on its own, has its own price, and would have its own photoIts own card on the menu, its own page
A required choiceThe customer must pick one before ordering, and it doesn't change the price — a rice type, a sauce, a fillingPick one. Order button stays locked until they do.
A variantIt changes the size or format and the price — small tray / large tray, 1kg / 2kgPick one, price updates. Effectively the size selector.
An add-onIt's extra, optional, and usually costs more — extra sauce, added bread, an upgradeTick any number. Each adds to the price.

The test I'll use

One product equals one thing a customer would point at and one photograph. If two menu lines would share the same photo and differ only in a detail chosen at ordering time, they're one product with a choice — not two products.

Two decisions this raises

Different choices, same dish. If a customer wants two trays of the same dish with different rice, that's two lines in the cart, not one line with quantity two. It's simple, it's honest, and the kitchen ticket is unambiguous.

Prices live on the product and the variant. Required choices default to no extra cost, add-ons default to a cost. Any of those can be overridden per choice when you send prices.

I need this before I can build quantities

For each category — Main Courses, Side Dishes, Salads — how is it sold and priced? Per tray, per kilo, per person, per piece, per whole dish, or as a package? And if by tray, what sizes exist and how many people does each serve? This one answer changes the product structure, the cart maths and the inventory design, so I'd rather ask than guess.

Three decisions worth explaining

Orders copy their own details. When an order is placed, the dish name, price and chosen options are written into the order itself — not just linked to the product. So when you raise a price next month, last month's orders still show what the customer actually paid. Without this, your revenue history quietly rewrites itself every time you edit the menu. This is the single most important structural decision here.

Money is stored as whole piastres. Computers are bad at decimal arithmetic in a way that produces one-piastre errors on totals. Storing 250.00 EGP as 25000 avoids it entirely. You'll never see this — you'll type prices in pounds.

Times are stored in Cairo time explicitly. The server is not in Egypt. A delivery date that shifts by a day because of a timezone assumption is the kind of bug that ruins a wedding, so it gets handled deliberately.

Section 07

What I'd build it with

The goal is boring, cheap and widely known — so that if you ever hand this to another developer, they recognise it immediately. Nothing here is exotic.

Next.jsThe website itself

Runs the customer site and the admin dashboard from one project, so there's one thing to deploy and one thing to update. Pages load fast on Egyptian mobile networks, and Google can read the menu properly — which matters for being found outside Instagram.

Tailwind CSSThe look

How the brand — the cream, the brown, the gold, the type — gets defined once and applied everywhere consistently. Changing an accent colour later is one line, not fifty.

PostgreSQLThe database

Where orders, products and customers actually live. It's the standard serious database, it handles money and dates correctly, and it will not be the thing you outgrow. Hosted by Neon or Supabase — free to start.

PrismaHow code talks to the database

Keeps the entire database structure in one readable file and manages changes to it safely. It's also what makes adding inventory later a controlled change rather than a risk.

Auth.jsAdmin login

Password login for the dashboard, with properly hashed passwords and sessions. Small, standard, and ready for staff accounts when you want them.

VercelWhere it lives

Publishes the site to the internet. Free HTTPS, a global network so it's fast from Egypt, and automatic backups of every version — so a bad change can be undone in one click.

Cloud storageYour photos

Product images uploaded from the dashboard, resized automatically so a 6MB phone photo doesn't make your menu slow to load on mobile data.

WhatsApp linkNotifications, for now

No API, no cost, no approval process. A button that opens WhatsApp with the message already written. The official Business API can replace it later without changing anything the customer sees.

On security

The admin dashboard is behind a password, and no page of it is reachable without one. Passwords are hashed, never stored readable. Nothing sensitive — database details, keys — ever goes into the code; it lives in a separate settings vault that only the running site can read. Order submission is rate-limited so nobody can flood you with fake orders. And no card details are ever stored, by anyone, at any point: when card payments are added, the payment provider handles that entirely, which is exactly how it should stay.
